How FinSight was built, and how far to trust it

Purpose and scope

FinSight is a research platform for financial news sentiment: given a sentence, is its tone positive, negative or neutral? It reads text only. It does not use prices or market data, does not forecast anything, and nothing it shows is investment advice.

Dataset

The corpus is Financial PhraseBank, sentences from English-language financial news labelled by finance-literate annotators. FinSight uses only the 50% agreement subset: a sentence is included when at least half the annotators agreed on its label. Other agreement levels are never mixed in.

What the exploratory analysis found

The full investigation lives in analysis/eda.ipynb with a written summary in analysis/eda_report.md. The findings that shaped the design:

  • Imbalance changes what “good” means. Always answering “neutral” scores about 59% accuracy. Models are therefore ranked by macro-F1.
  • Length is not a signal. Mean sentence length is about {{ dataset.mean_words }} words; class differences are statistically detectable but tiny (effect size 0.013).
  • Numbers matter. About 75% of negative sentences contain digits, against 47% of neutral ones, so numbers and the % sign are kept as features.
  • Direction words carry sentiment; money words are ambiguous. “Loss” appears about equally in positive and negative sentences (“loss narrowed”). Context and word order matter.
  • Labels are genuinely uncertain. On sentences where annotators were not unanimous, a quick check scored far lower (macro-F1 about 0.59) than on unanimous ones (about 0.88).
  • Stopword removal hurts. Standard stopword lists delete words like “not”, “down” and “against”; removing them lowered validation macro-F1 from about 0.73 to 0.64, so FinSight keeps them.

Text features

Text is lower-cased and whitespace-normalised, then converted with TF-IDF (term frequency × inverse document frequency), which up-weights words that are distinctive and down-weights words that appear everywhere. The vectoriser uses single words and two-word phrases and is fitted inside the model pipeline, only ever on training data, so nothing leaks from validation or test.

The limitation of this representation is that word order is largely lost: “loss narrowed” and “loss widened” differ by one word, and only two-word phrases partly capture such patterns.

Experiment design

  • One stratified 70 / 15 / 15 split into training, validation and test sets, with a fixed seed ({{ meta.random_seed if meta else 42 }}); every model sees identical rows.
  • Six candidates share one registry and one pipeline shape: a majority-class baseline, Multinomial and Complement Naive Bayes, Logistic Regression, a linear SVM and an SGD classifier.
  • Each candidate has a small hyper-parameter grid. 5-fold cross-validation on the training split is recorded, and validation macro-F1 selects the setting and the winner. Ties fall back to CV score, then speed.
  • The test set was untouched until selection was finished. The winner was then refit on training + validation and scored once on the test set.

Scores and explanations

The selected model is a linear SVM, which produces decision scores, not probabilities, and FinSight never presents them as probabilities. To give a usable sense of confidence it reports a margin band: the gap between the top two class scores, grouped into Low / Medium / High, with the real accuracy of each band measured out-of-fold on the training and validation data.

Word explanations multiply each word’s TF-IDF weight by the model coefficient for the predicted class. They show statistical association learned from training data — not causation or understanding — and they ignore longer-range context.

Limitations

  • Narrow data. About 4,800 sentences from one annotated collection of mostly Nordic company news from the 2000s, in English. Other domains, regions and writing styles may behave differently.
  • Subjective labels. Annotators disagreed on many sentences; no model can fully resolve that. Annotator-level labels are not published, so inter-annotator reliability (for example Cohen’s κ) is not computed here.
  • No context. Each sentence is judged alone, without the company, date or market expectations that a human analyst would use.
  • Neutral versus polar is hard. Positive sentences are most often mistaken for neutral.
  • Bag-of-words ceiling. Negation and long-range word order are only partly captured.
  • Not a trading tool. Sentiment of a sentence is not a forecast and not advice.
